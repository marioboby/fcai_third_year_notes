## 1. Neuroimaging Modality Selection: The Physics and Trade-offs of EEG

Before we place a single electrode on the scalp, we must answer a fundamental engineering question: _Why use EEG at all, and when should we abandon it for another neuroimaging modality?_

### 1.1 The Spatio-Temporal Resolution Trade-off

- **Electroencephalography (EEG)** is the electrophysiological monitoring method used to record electrical activity generated by synchronized post-synaptic potentials of cortical pyramidal neurons using non-invasive scalp electrodes.
<br>
> [!يعني ايه بقي كل الكلام الحمصي ده]
> An **EEG** (electroencephalogram) is a test that tracks brain waves.
> 
> Sensors (electrodes) stick to the scalp to detect the tiny electrical pulses brain cells use to talk to each other, recording the patterns onto a screen. Doctors use it to spot abnormal brain activity, such as during seizures, sleep issues, or head injuries.

- **Temporal Resolution** refers to the precision of a measurement with respect to time—specifically, how rapidly a system can sample changes in brain activity.
    
      
    - EEG possesses **exceptional temporal resolution** (on the order of milliseconds, sampling hundreds or thousands of times per second). Because electrical fields propagate through biological tissue at nearly the speed of light, scalp electrodes capture neural firing dynamics virtually instantaneously.
 <br>
- **Spatial Resolution** refers to the precision with which a neuroimaging technique can localize the exact anatomical origin of neural activity within the three-dimensional volume of the brain.
    
      
    - EEG suffers from **poor spatial resolution** (on the order of centimeters). As electrical currents travel from the cerebral cortex through the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), the highly resistive skull, and the conductive scalp, the skull acts as a spatial low-pass filter, smearing and attenuating the electrical potential distribution across a wide surface area.

هتلاقوا معني الاتنين بشكل ابسط [[4th Year/1st Term/BCI/Before Mid/Lecture 1 - Intro#EEG Capabilities and Challenges\|المحاضرة اللي فاتت]]
<br>


- **Modality Selection Criteria ("Why EEG? It depends on the purpose..."):**
    
      
    - **Core Engineering Advantage:** EEG is chosen for BCI applications because it maximizes **temporal resolution**, minimizes hardware **cost**, and maintains portability, whereas modalities like **Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI)** or **Positron Emission Tomography (PET)** offer high spatial resolution (millimeters) at the expense of slow temporal resolution (seconds, due to the sluggish hemodynamic response) and massive financial/infrastructural cost.
    <br>
    - **Example:** If you are building a real-time **P300 Speller** or a motor imagery prosthetic controller, you need to detect a neural response within $300\text{ ms}$ of a visual cue. An fMRI scanner takes $2\text{ to }6\text{ seconds}$ just to register blood flow changes (the BOLD signal), making it useless for rapid communication, whereas a portable EEG headset captures the exact millisecond deflection of the cortical potential.
        
          
        

### 1.2 Volume Conduction, The Inverse Problem, and Jittered Trials

- **Volume Conduction** is the physical process by which electrical potentials generated by a localized population of neurons spread passively through the conductive tissues of the brain, skull, and scalp to reach multiple distant sensors simultaneously.
    
      
    - Because of volume conduction, a single cortical source does not just appear on the electrode directly above it; its electrical field is picked up as a linear mixture across _all_ scalp sensors (e.g., across a $32\text{-sensor}$ or $100\text{-sensor}$ array).
  <br>
- **The Electromagnetic Inverse Problem** is the mathematical task of estimating the exact internal three-dimensional cortical current sources ($N_{\text{sources}}$) given only the two-dimensional electrical potential measurements recorded on the scalp surface ($N_{\text{sensors}}$).
    
      
    - **Fundamental Mathematical Law (Ill-Posed Nature of the Inverse Problem):** While the **Forward Problem** (predicting scalp potentials when you already know the exact internal brain sources) has a unique, deterministic physical solution via Maxwell's equations, the **Inverse Problem** is mathematically **ill-posed** (non-unique). Because the number of microscopic neural dipoles in the brain ($>10^9$) vastly exceeds the number of scalp sensors (e.g., $32\text{ or }100\text{ sensors}$), an infinite number of different internal source configurations can produce the exact same voltage topography on the scalp.
    <br>
    - Look at the simple algebraic intuition noted in the lecture slides: $3 + 6 = 9$. If I tell you the inputs are $3$ and $6$, you know the output at the sensor is uniquely $9$ (the Forward Problem). However, if you only measure the sensor output $9$ and must guess the two internal brain sources that summed to create it (the Inverse Problem), is the answer $3 + 6$? Or $4 + 5$? Or $10 + (-1)$? Without adding strict anatomical and mathematical constraints, you cannot uniquely solve it!
    <br>
- **Independent Component Analysis (ICA)** is a computational blind source separation algorithm used in EEG preprocessing to unmix multivariate scalp signals into maximally statistically independent subcomponents.
    
      
    - Because scalp electrodes record a linear mixture of brain sources and physiological artifacts ($\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{S}$, where $\mathbf{X}$ is recorded data, $\mathbf{A}$ is the spatial mixing matrix, and $\mathbf{S}$ represents the underlying independent sources), ICA estimates an unmixing matrix $\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{A}^{-1}$ to isolate pure cortical sources from artifacts like eye blinks.
    <br>
- **Trial Jitter (Temporal Misalignment)** occurs when the latency between an external experimental stimulus (trigger) and the brain's internal neural response varies unpredictably from trial to trial.
    
      
    - When trials are **phase-locked** and aligned in time, simple averaging across trials cancels out random background noise and reveals clean time-domain waveforms.
    <br>
    - However, when **trials are jittered** (e.g., a subject takes $300\text{ ms}$ to recognize a complex image on Trial 1, $450\text{ ms}$ on Trial 2, and $600\text{ ms}$ on Trial 3), averaging the signals in the time domain causes the peaks and troughs of the waves to cancel each other out, destroying the signal!
    <br>
    - Furthermore, when the **frequency is very low** (slow cortical potentials or infra-slow oscillations $< 0.5\text{ Hz}$), skin potentials, sweat artifacts, and DC amplifier drift easily corrupt standard time-domain EEG. In scenarios involving severe trial jitter, deep subcortical localization, or ultra-low frequency metabolic tracking, standard time-averaged ERP analysis fails, requiring either **time-frequency analysis** (computing power before averaging across trials) or alternative neuroimaging measures (such as fMRI or MEG).
    <br>
    - **Case Study:** Imagine recording $100\text{ trials}$ of high-frequency gamma oscillations ($40\text{ Hz}$) induced by cognitive decision-making. Because decision-making onset varies by $\pm 50\text{ ms}$ across trials (jitter), a standard time-domain average yields a flat line. By switching from time-domain averaging to spectral power analysis (or using ICA to isolate the active parietal component first), the researcher recovers the robust $40\text{ Hz}$ burst on every single trial.
        
          
        
> [!يعني ايه الهم ده كله]
> **Trial jitter** happens when your brain doesn't react at the exact same split-second every time you run a test.
> 
>   
> 
> - **When timing is identical:** If your brain reacts at exactly 300 ms every single time, averaging 100 trials stacks the wave peaks together, cancels background noise, and gives a sharp, clear signal.
> <br>
> - **When timing is jittered:** If your brain takes 300 ms on test 1, 450 ms on test 2, and 600 ms on test 3, the waves don't line up. When you average them, the peaks and troughs cancel each other out, leaving a flat line—wiping out the real brain activity.
>   <br>
> - **Why slow waves make it worse:** Extremely slow brain waves (under 0.5 Hz) are easily ruined by simple bodily noise like sweat, skin tension, or equipment drift, making regular time-averaging completely useless.
> <br>
> - **The fix:** Instead of directly averaging the raw waves (time-domain), researchers measure the **strength (power)** of the waves on each individual trial first, and then average that power. That way, it doesn't matter if the burst happens early or late—its energy is preserved.
## 2. Electrode Placement, Montages, and Spatial Re-Referencing

A single electrode on the scalp cannot measure "absolute brain voltage" because voltage, by physical definition, is an **electrical potential difference** between two points ($V_{AB} = V_A - V_B$). Therefore, every single channel in an EEG recording is actually a differential measurement comparing an active scalp site against a chosen reference site.

  

### 2.1 The International 10–20 System & Scalp Topography

- **The International 10–20 System** is the globally standardized anatomical method for positioning scalp electrodes based on proportional distances ($10\%$ and $20\%$) between fixed cranial landmarks, ensuring reproducible coverage over underlying cortical lobes regardless of head size or shape.
<br>
- **Cranial Landmarks:**
    
      
    - **Nasion ($Nz$):** The distinct depressed area directly between the eyes, just superior to the bridge of the nose (the anterior anatomical anchor).
    <br>
    - **Inion ($Iz$):** The most prominent projection of the occipital bone at the lower rear base of the skull (the posterior anatomical anchor).
    <br>
    - **Pre-auricular Points / Mastoids ($M_1, M_2$ or $A_1, A_2$):** The bony prominences located directly behind the left and right ears (the lateral anatomical anchors, frequently used as electrically quiet reference points).
    <br>
- **Proportional Spacing Mechanics:**
    
      
    - If you measure the total (arc) distance along the midline of the skull from the **Nasion** to the **Inion** as $100\%$, the first prefrontal electrode plane ($Fpz$) is placed at $10\%$ of that total distance above the Nasion.
    <br>
    - Subsequent coronal rows—Frontal ($Fz$), Central ($Cz$), Parietal ($Pz$), and Occipital ($Oz$)—are spaced at exactly $20\%$ intervals along the midline, with the final $10\%$ gap separating the Occipital plane ($Oz$) from the **Inion** ($10\% - 20\% - 20\% - 20\% - 20\% - 10\% = 100\%$).
    <br>
    - **Nomenclature Convention:** Letters denote the anatomical lobe (**F**rontal, **T**emporal, **C**entral, **P**arietal, **O**ccipital), **z** (zero) denotes midline electrodes ($Fz, Cz, Pz$), **odd numbers** ($1, 3, 5, 7$) denote the **left hemisphere**, and **even numbers** ($2, 4, 6, 8$) denote the **right hemisphere**.
   <br>
- **Scalp Topography (Topographic Mapping)** is the two-dimensional spatial visualization of electrical potential distributions (or spectral power) projected onto a stylized circular head model, using color gradients and contour lines to interpolate values between discrete electrodes (e.g., $32\text{ sensors}$).
    
      
    - **Example:** During a motor imagery task, plotting the scalp topography at $t = 500\text{ ms}$ reveals a localized blue focal spot (voltage suppression) directly over electrode $C_3$ (left motor cortex) when the user imagines moving their right hand.
        
          
        

### 2.2 Hardware Terminology: Sensors, Electrodes, and Channels

Students frequently conflate sensors, electrodes, and channels, which leads to major bugs when writing signal processing code. Let us establish the strict distinction:

  

- **Electrode / Sensor:** The physical conductive disk (e.g., $\text{Ag/AgCl}$ ring) placed on the scalp to transduce ionic current in the tissue into electronic current in the wire.
<br>
- **Channel:** A single recorded time-series signal resulting from the algebraic subtraction of two physical electrodes (an active electrode minus a reference electrode) amplified by a differential amplifier.
    
      
    - **Critical Distinction:** You can have $33\text{ physical electrodes}$ on a cap ($32\text{ active sensors} + 1\text{ hardware reference sensor}$), which yields $32\text{ data channels}$ in your software matrix.
        
          
        

### 2.3 EEG Montages and Re-Referencing Transformations

- **An EEG Montage** is the specific logical and mathematical configuration defining how electrode pairs are combined and referenced to display and analyze channels across the scalp.
 <br>
- **Re-Referencing** is the offline linear algebraic transformation applied to recorded EEG channels to change the spatial reference point after data acquisition is complete, optimizing the signal-to-noise ratio for specific cortical features.
    
      
    

Let $V_i(t)$ be the true electrical potential at scalp site $i$ at time $t$, and let $V_{\text{ref}}(t)$ be the potential at the physical hardware reference site during recording. The raw recorded signal at channel $i$ is:

  

$$X_i(t) = V_i(t) - V_{\text{ref}}(t)$$

We categorize montages and re-referencing schemes into four primary paradigms covered in the course:

  

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                        EEG MONTAGE & REFERENCING TAXONOMY                   |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 1. UNIPOLAR / COMMON REFERENCE (e.g., Cz Reference, Mastoid M1/M2)          |
|    [Electrode i] --------(+)--> [Diff Amp] ---> Channel i = V_i - V_ref     |
|    [Common Ref ] --------(-)-->                                             |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 2. BIPOLAR MONTAGE (Sequential Adjacent Chains)                             |
|    [Electrode 1] --(+)--> [Amp 1] -> Ch 1 = V_1 - V_2                       |
|    [Electrode 2] --(-)-->                                                   |
|    [Electrode 2] --(+)--> [Amp 2] -> Ch 2 = V_2 - V_3                       |
|    [Electrode 3] --(-)-->                                                   |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 3. COMMON AVERAGE REFERENCE (CAR)                                           |
|    Ch_i^(CAR) = X_i - (1/N) * Sum_{j=1..N}(X_j)                             |
|    (Subtracts global mean across all N electrodes to cancel global noise)   |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 4. LAPLACIAN MONTAGE (Surface Spatial High-Pass Filter)                     |
|    Ch_i^(Lap) = X_i - (1/K) * Sum_{k in Neighbors(i)}(X_k)                  |
|    (Subtracts local surrounding ring to isolate sharp radial sources)       |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

#### 1. Reference Montage (Unipolar / Common Reference: $Cz$ or Mastoids)

- **Mechanism:** Every active electrode $i \in \{1, \dots, N\}$ is measured relative to a single shared physical electrode—most commonly the vertex electrode **$Cz$**, a single ear/mastoid ($M_1$ or $M_2$), or **linked mastoids** ($\frac{M_1 + M_2}{2}$).
    
      
    
- **Mathematical Formulation:**
    
      
    
    $$X_i^{\text{Ref}}(t) = V_i(t) - V_{Cz}(t)$$
    
- **Nuances & Limitations:**
    
      
    - There is **no truly electrically neutral ("inactive") site on the human body**. If you use **$Cz$ as your reference**, any actual brain activity occurring underneath $Cz$ (such as motor or auditory evoked potentials) is subtracted from _every single channel_ in your dataset, artificially injecting inverted $Cz$ activity across the entire scalp!
   <br>
    - Conversely, electrodes physically very close to $Cz$ (like $C_1, C_2, FCz, CPz$) will show artificially tiny amplitudes because $V_i(t) \approx V_{Cz}(t)$, leading to near-zero difference.
    <br>
    - **Mastoid references ($M_1/M_2$)** are further from cortical tissue, reducing brain signal contamination, but are susceptible to neck muscle (**EMG**) artifacts and electrocardiogram (**ECG**) pulse artifacts.
<br>
- **Example:** If you record a $32\text{-channel}$ dataset using a **$Cz$ reference montage** during a foot motor imagery experiment (where the foot representation lies right under the midline vertex $Cz$), subtracting $V_{Cz}$ will accidentally erase your target signal from the surrounding central channels! You must re-reference offline to recover it.
    
      
    

#### 2. Bipolar Montage

- **Mechanism:** Instead of sharing a single common reference, channels are formed by linking sequential pairs of adjacent electrodes along longitudinal (anterior-to-posterior, e.g., "double banana") or transverse (left-to-right) chains, where the negative reference input of Channel 1 becomes the positive active input of Channel 2.
  <br>
- **Mathematical Formulation:**
    
      
    
    $$\text{Channel}_{1,2}(t) = X_1(t) - X_2(t) = (V_1(t) - V_{\text{ref}}(t)) - (V_2(t) - V_{\text{ref}}(t)) = V_1(t) - V_2(t)$$
    
    (Notice how the original hardware reference $V_{\text{ref}}(t)$ mathematically cancels out completely!)
  <br>
- **Nuances & Limitations:**
    
      
    - Bipolar montages provide sharp local spatial differentiation and reject widespread common-mode interference, making them the clinical gold standard in epileptology for locating **phase reversals** (where two adjacent bipolar channels point toward each other, pinpointing a focal seizure discharge at the shared middle electrode).
    <br>
    - However, they distort broad, distributed cortical waveforms (like the P300) because any potential shared equally between two adjacent electrodes is subtracted to zero.
    <br>
- **Example:** In a longitudinal bipolar chain ($Fp_1 - F_3$, $F_3 - C_3$, $C_3 - P_3$), if a focal motor spike occurs squarely beneath $C_3$, channel $(F_3 - C_3)$ will deflect upward while channel $(C_3 - P_3)$ deflects downward—creating a classic bipolar "phase reversal" pointing directly at $C_3$.
    
      
    

#### 3. Common Average Reference (CAR)

- **Mechanism:** **Common Average Referencing (CAR)** is an offline spatial re-referencing technique that computes the instantaneous arithmetic mean of all $N$ recorded scalp channels at time point $t$ and subtracts this global average from each individual channel.
    
      
    
- **Mathematical Formulation & Proof of Reference Cancellation:**
    
      
    - First, compute the average across all $N$ recorded channels (which were originally recorded against hardware reference $V_{\text{ref}}$):
        
          
        
        $$\bar{X}(t) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} X_j(t) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} \left( V_j(t) - V_{\text{ref}}(t) \right) = \left( \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} V_j(t) \right) - V_{\text{ref}}(t)$$
        
    - Next, subtract this global mean $\bar{X}(t)$ from channel $X_i(t)$:
        
          
        
        $$X_i^{\text{CAR}}(t) = X_i(t) - \bar{X}(t) = \left( V_i(t) - V_{\text{ref}}(t) \right) - \left[ \left( \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} V_j(t) \right) - V_{\text{ref}}(t) \right]$$
        
        $$X_i^{\text{CAR}}(t) = V_i(t) - \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} V_j(t)$$
        
- **Theoretical Justification (Bertrand’s Theorem / Quasi-Neutrality):**
    
      
    - In physics, the total surface integral of electrical potentials over a closed, homogeneous volume conductor containing internal current dipoles equals zero ($\oint V \, dS = 0$).
        
          
        
    - Therefore, if you have a sufficiently large number of electrodes (e.g., $N \ge 32$ or $64$) distributed evenly across the scalp, the sample mean $\frac{1}{N}\sum V_j(t)$ approximates zero, providing a **reference-free**, virtual zero-potential baseline!
        
          
        
    - **Critical Warning:** Before computing CAR, you **must** remove or interpolate noisy/broken channels and massive high-amplitude artifacts. If a single electrode has a loose wire with a $500\text{ }\mu\text{V}$ spike, including it in the average $\frac{1}{N}\sum X_j(t)$ will inject $-\frac{500}{N}\text{ }\mu\text{V}$ of artificial noise into _every single clean channel_ in your dataset!
        
          
        
- **Example:** In a $32\text{-channel}$ BCI headset operating in a noisy laboratory environment where $50\text{ Hz}$ power-line interference affects all wires equally, applying **CAR** subtracts out the shared ambient electrical hum while preserving the localized cortical differences.
    
      
    

#### 4. Laplacian Montage (Surface Laplacian / Current Source Density)

- **Mechanism:** The **Laplacian Montage** acts as a **spatial high-pass filter** by subtracting the weighted average of only the _immediate surrounding neighbor electrodes_ from each central electrode, estimating the local radial current density flowing perpendicularly through the skull directly beneath that sensor.
    
      
    
- **Mathematical Formulation:**
    
      
    - Analytically, the Surface Laplacian is proportional to the negated second spatial derivative of the scalp potential field $V(x, y)$:
        
          
        
        $$\nabla^2_s V = \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial y^2}$$
        
    - In its discrete **Hjorth Approximation** (finite-difference form for equally spaced neighboring electrodes $k \in \mathcal{N}_i$, typically the $K = 4$ nearest orthogonal neighbors at equal distance $d$):
        
          
        
        $$X_i^{\text{Lap}}(t) = X_i(t) - \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k \in \mathcal{N}_i} X_k(t)$$
        
    - Notice how this discrete operator corresponds to a $2\text{D}$ spatial convolution kernel where the center electrode has weight $+1$ (or $+4$ before normalization) and its four immediate orthogonal neighbors have weights $-0.25$ (or $-1$ each).
        
          
        
- **Nuances & Limitations:**
    
      
    - Because it subtracts the immediate local neighborhood, the Laplacian montage aggressively attenuates deep, broad, volume-conducted signals (low spatial frequencies) and sharply highlights focal, superficial radial cortical sources (high spatial frequencies).
        
          
        
    - **Boundary Problem:** Electrodes on the outer perimeter of the cap (e.g., $Fp_1, O_2, T_7, T_8$) lack a full ring of surrounding neighbors, making discrete Laplacian estimates inaccurate at the edges of the array unless spherical spline interpolation is used.
        
          
        
- **Analogy:** Think of **CAR** vs. **Laplacian** like image processing filters. **CAR** is like subtracting the overall brightness of the entire photograph so the lighting is balanced globally, whereas the **Laplacian montage** is an **edge-detection sharpening kernel** that erases smooth background gradients to make sharp local features pop out over the motor cortex (such as computing $C_3^{\text{Lap}} = C_3 - \frac{1}{4}(F_3 + P_3 + Cz + T_7)$).

## 3. Physiology of Neural Rhythms and Behavioral States

Why does raw EEG look like a chaotic superposition of jagged waves, and why does its appearance change dramatically between **Wake** and **Sleep** states? The answer lies in **neural synchrony** and **neocortical dynamics** (as classically formalized in Paul Nunez’s _Neocortical Dynamics and Human EEG Rhythms_, 1995, referenced in the lecture slides).

  

### 3.1 Synchrony vs. Desynchrony: The "Why?" of Wake and Sleep

- **Macroscopic Post-Synaptic Summation:** Scalp EEG does _not_ primarily measure individual **action potentials** (spikes), because action potentials are ultra-fast ($\sim 1\text{ ms}$) and symmetric quadrupole fields that rapidly cancel out over distance. Instead, EEG measures slow **Excitatory and Inhibitory Post-Synaptic Potentials (EPSPs and IPSPs)** lasting $10\text{–}100\text{ ms}$ across thousands of vertically aligned cortical **pyramidal neurons** acting as open electrical dipoles.
<br>
- **Desynchronized Activity (Wakefulness / Active Cognition):**
    
      
    - When a person is awake, alert, and actively processing sensory information, different cortical micro-columns fire independently to process distinct tasks.
    <br>
    - Because millions of small neural populations are oscillating out of phase with one another, their electrical peaks and troughs partially cancel out at the scalp, resulting in **low-amplitude, high-frequency** waveforms (Beta and Gamma rhythms).
  <br>
- **Synchronized Activity (Drowsiness / Deep Sleep):**
    
      
    - During deep slow-wave sleep, thalamocortical pacemaker loops drive millions of cortical neurons to fire and pause in massive, rhythmic unison.
    <br>
    - Because their post-synaptic potentials sum constructively at the exact same instant, the scalp electrode records **high-amplitude, low-frequency** waveforms (Delta and Theta rhythms).
 <br>
- **Analogy:** Imagine a stadium holding $50,000$ spectators (cortical neurons) with a single microphone suspended high above the roof (the scalp electrode). During **Wakefulness**, every spectator is having a private, complex conversation with their neighbor; the microphone records a quiet, fast, high-frequency "buzz" of white noise because the voices cancel out. During **Deep Sleep**, all $50,000$ people stop talking and begin chanting a slow, rhythmic two-syllable chant in perfect unison; the microphone now records a deafeningly loud (high-amplitude), slow (low-frequency) wave!

### 3.2 Canonical EEG Frequency Bands

Neural oscillations are partitioned into five canonical frequency bands based on their spectral frequency (measured in **Hertz, $\text{Hz}$**, or cycles per second) and functional neurophysiology:

  

| **Frequency Band**   | **Frequency Range ($\text{Hz}$)** | **Amplitude & Morphology**                                              | **Primary Cortical / Behavioral Correlates**                                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Delta ($\delta$)** | $0 - 4\text{ Hz}$                 | Highest amplitude, slow rolling waves                                   | Deep slow-wave sleep (**N3**), comatose states, or pathological focal lesions in waking adults.                                                                                                                                                    |
| **Theta ($\theta$)** | $4 - 7\text{ Hz}$                 | Medium-to-high amplitude, rhythmic                                      | Drowsiness, early sleep (**N1**), working memory load (frontal midline theta), and hippocampal spatial navigation.                                                                                                                                 |
| **Alpha ($\alpha$)** | $8 - 12\text{ Hz}$                | Regular, sinusoidal waves; prominent over occipital cortex ($O_1, O_2$) | Relaxed wakefulness with **eyes closed**; exhibits **Alpha Blocking** (attenuation) upon eye opening or visual attention. _(Note: Over the sensorimotor strip $C_3/C_4$, the $8\text{–}12\text{ Hz}$ rhythm is termed the **Mu ($\mu$) rhythm**)_. |
| **Beta ($\beta$)**   | $12 - 30\text{ Hz}$               | Low amplitude, fast irregular oscillations                              | Active, alert wakefulness, focused cognitive concentration, anxious thinking, and active motor control/inhibition.                                                                                                                                 |
| **Gamma ($\gamma$)** | $30 - 100+\text{ Hz}$             | Very low amplitude, ultra-fast oscillations                             | Cross-modal sensory binding, high-level working memory, conscious perception, and localized cortical computation.                                                                                                                                  |

- **Example:** In a motor imagery BCI, when a user is resting, their sensorimotor cortex exhibits a strong $10\text{ Hz}$ **Alpha/Mu ($\alpha/\mu$)** idling rhythm. The moment they imagine clenching their fist, the motor cortex transitions from idling to active processing, causing a sharp drop in $8\text{–}12\text{ Hz}$ power (**Event-Related Desynchronization, ERD**) accompanied by high-frequency **Beta/Gamma** activity.
    
      
    

### 3.3 Sleep Architecture & Polysomnographic Signatures

As noted in the lecture's sleep rhythm breakdown, human sleep cycles through distinct **Non-Rapid Eye Movement (NREM)** stages and **Rapid Eye Movement (REM)** sleep roughly every $90\text{ minutes}$:

  

- **Wakefulness ($W$):** Dominated by fast, low-voltage **Beta** ($12\text{–}30\text{ Hz}$) with eyes open, transitioning to posterior **Alpha** ($8\text{–}12\text{ Hz}$) when eyes close.
<br>
- **Stage N1 (Light Transitional Sleep):** **Alpha** waves drop out and are replaced by low-amplitude, mixed-frequency **Theta** ($4\text{–}7\text{ Hz}$) activity and slow rolling eye movements.
<br>
- **Stage N2 (Consolidated Light Sleep):** Characterized by a **Theta** background punctuated by two iconic transient waveforms:
    
      
    - **Sleep Spindles:** Sudden, waxing-and-waning bursts of $11\text{–}16\text{ Hz}$ (sigma-band) sinusoidal oscillations lasting $0.5\text{ to }2\text{ seconds}$, generated by thalamocortical circuits to gate external sensory input and consolidate memory.
    <br>
    - **K-Complexes:** Sharp, high-amplitude biphasic waves (a sudden negative deflection followed by a positive deflection) lasting $> 0.5\text{ seconds}$.
   <br>
- **Stage N3 (Deep Slow-Wave Sleep):** Dominated by high-amplitude ($> 75\text{ }\mu\text{V}$), slow **Delta** ($0.5\text{–}4\text{ Hz}$) oscillations covering at least $20\%$ of a $30\text{-second}$ epoch.
<br>
- **REM Sleep (Rapid Eye Movement / Paradoxical Sleep):**
    
      
    - Known as "paradoxical sleep" because the EEG suddenly returns to a **desynchronized, low-voltage, mixed-frequency state** (resembling Stage N1 or active Wakefulness with Theta and Beta components, plus characteristic "sawtooth waves").
    <br>
    - Simultaneously, the **Electrooculogram (EOG)** records rapid, conjugate darting eye movements, while skeletal muscle tone is completely paralyzed (atonia) as vivid dreaming occurs.
<br>
    - **Case Study:** If an automated sleep-staging classifier only looks at single-channel EEG spectral power, it will frequently misclassify **REM sleep** as **Wake** or **N1** because their frequency spectra overlap. Incorporating **EOG** channels (to detect rapid eye movements) and **Spindle** detectors (to isolate N2) resolves the ambiguity.
        
          
        

## 4. Multivariate EEG Data Formatting, Experimental Paradigms, and Artifacts

When we acquire data from multiple sensors over an experimental session, how do we structure continuous electrical streams into clean mathematical arrays suitable for machine learning and statistical testing?

  

### 4.1 The Multivariate Dimensions of EEG Signals

EEG is fundamentally **multivariate time-series data** governed by four core physical and analytical dimensions:

  

1. **Space ($Channels / Sensors$):** The discrete anatomical coordinates across the scalp ($C$ channels, e.g., $C = 32$ or $64$) capturing spatial topography.
    
      
    
2. **Time ($t$ / $Samples$):** The chronological evolution of voltage potential sampled at discrete intervals.
    
      
    
3. **Frequency ($f$):** The spectral rate of oscillation (in $\text{Hz}$) extracted via sinusoidal basis projections.
    
      
    
4. **Phase ($\phi$):** The instantaneous angular position ($0\text{ to }2\pi\text{ radians}$) of an oscillation within its periodic cycle at a given point in time.
    
      
    

### 4.2 Sampling Rate ($F_s$), Discretization, and Temporal Indexing

- **Sampling Rate ($F_s$)** is the number of discrete voltage measurements ("samples") acquired per second from each analog EEG channel by the Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC), measured in **Hertz ($\text{Hz}$)** (analogous to frames per second, $\text{fps}$, in video acquisition).
<br>
- **Fundamental Conversion Formulas:**
    
      
    - Given a sampling rate $F_s$ (e.g., $F_s = 100\text{ Hz}$ as illustrated on Slide 16), the **Sampling Period ($\Delta t$)**—the exact time elapsed between two consecutive samples—is:
        
          
        
        $$\Delta t = \frac{1}{F_s} \text{ seconds}$$
        
    - For a recording window of duration $T_{\text{sec}}$ seconds, the total number of discrete digital **Samples ($N_{\text{samples}}$)** is:
        
          
        
        $$N_{\text{samples}} = T_{\text{sec}} \times F_s$$
        
    - Conversely, to convert a discrete sample index $n \in \{0, 1, \dots, N_{\text{samples}}-1\}$ back into physical time $t$ (in seconds):
        
          
        
        $$t_n = \frac{n}{F_s} = n \cdot \Delta t$$
        
- **Nyquist-Shannon Sampling Theorem:** To accurately reconstruct a continuous signal without aliasing distortion, the sampling rate $F_s$ must be strictly greater than twice the highest frequency component ($f_{\text{max}}$) present in the signal ($F_s > 2 f_{\text{max}}$, where $f_{\text{Nyquist}} = \frac{F_s}{2}$).
<br>
- **Example (From Lecture Slide 5 & 16):** Suppose your EEG amplifier operates at a sampling rate of $F_s = 100\text{ Hz}$. Each sample is separated by $\Delta t = \frac{1}{100} = 0.01\text{ seconds} = 10\text{ ms}$. If a trial window lasts $0.8\text{ seconds}$ ($800\text{ ms}$), that trial contains exactly $0.8\text{ s} \times 100\text{ Hz} = 80\text{ discrete samples}$ per channel. If an entire experimental trial period lasts $4\text{ seconds}$, each channel vector for that trial contains $4\text{ s} \times 100\text{ Hz} = 400\text{ samples}$!
    
      
    

### 4.3 Experimental Trial Structure: Triggers, Cues, Events, and ERPs

In BCI experiments, we rarely analyze hours of unsegmented continuous EEG. Instead, we lock our analysis to controlled experimental milestones:

  

```
|<----------------------------------- Total Trial Period (e.g., 4 sec) ----------------------------------->|
|                                                                                                          |
|   Baseline / Pre-Stimulus    Trigger / Cue          Task Execution / ERP Window          Rest / ITI      |
|----------------------------|---------------|-------------------------------------------|-----------------|
                             ^               |<-------- e.g., 800 ms (0.8 sec) --------->|
                             |               ^                                           ^
                          t = 0 ms        t = +200 ms                                 t = +1000 ms
                       [Stimulus Onset]   (Task Start)                                (Task End)
                       ("Left" / "Right")
```

- **Trigger / Event Marker:** A precise digital timestamp pulse (e.g., sent via parallel port or Lab Streaming Layer, LSL) recorded simultaneously alongside the EEG channels to mark the exact millisecond ($t = 0$) an external event occurs.
<br>
- **Cue / Stimulus:** The sensory presentation shown to the subject (e.g., an arrow pointing **Left** or **Right** on a monitor, a flashing letter in a **P300 Speller**, or an emotional image) that instructs the subject to perform a mental task or elicits an automatic neural response.
<br>
- **Epoching (Time-Locking):** The preprocessing step of slicing a continuous $2\text{D}$ EEG recording ($Channels \times Total\_Time$) into fixed-length time windows (**epochs** or **trials**) anchored around each stimulus trigger (e.g., extracting from $t = 0\text{ s}$ to $t = 4\text{ s}$, or focusing on an active window of $800\text{ ms}$ / $0.8\text{ s}$).
<br>
- **Event-Related Potential (ERP):** A stereotyped, **time-locked** and **phase-locked** voltage deflection in the EEG waveform that occurs at a fixed latency following a specific sensory, cognitive, or motor event.
    
      
    - Because a single-trial ERP (a few microvolts, $\mu\text{V}$) is buried inside much larger background EEG oscillations ($\pm 50\text{ }\mu\text{V}$), extracting an ERP requires averaging across $M$ repeated trials at each time sample $t$:
        
          
        
        $$\text{ERP}_c(t) = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} X_{c, t, m}$$
        
    - By the Law of Large Numbers, random background noise (assumed zero-mean Gaussian with variance $\sigma^2$) decreases in amplitude by a factor of $\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}$, while the time-locked ERP waveform remains constant and emerges clearly.
        
          
        
- **The P300 Speller Paradigm:**
    
      
    - The **P300** is a positive-going ERP deflection peaking roughly $300\text{ ms}$ after a rare, task-relevant stimulus (elicited via an **Oddball Paradigm**) with maximal amplitude over central-parietal midline electrodes ($Cz, Pz$).
        
          
        
    - In a **P300 Speller**, a user stares at a $6 \times 6$ matrix of characters on a screen while rows and columns flash rapidly in random order. Whenever the specific row or column containing the user's desired letter flashes (the rare target event), the user's brain automatically fires a **P300** wave; by intersecting the row and column that elicited the P300, the BCI types the character without any muscle movement!
        
          
        
- **Artifact Contamination — Electrooculography (EOG):**
    
      
    - The human eyeball acts as a strong electrical dipole (with a positively charged cornea and a negatively charged retina).
        
          
        
    - When a subject blinks or moves their eyes (**Transition: Left $\leftrightarrow$ Right**), this massive ocular dipole rotates, generating **Electrooculogram (EOG) noise** that is $10\text{ to }100\text{ times}$ larger in amplitude ($\sim 100\text{–}500\text{ }\mu\text{V}$) than true cortical EEG ($\sim 5\text{–}50\text{ }\mu\text{V}$)!
        
          
        
    - **Critical BCI Pitfall:** In a paradigm where a visual cue instructs the user to imagine **Left** vs. **Right** movement (or look at left vs. right stimuli), if the subject accidentally shifts their eyes left or right, a naive machine learning classifier will achieve $95\%$ accuracy by classifying the massive frontal **EOG eye-movement artifact** rather than actual brain activity! We must record dedicated EOG channels or apply ICA to strip ocular noise before classification.
        
          
        

### 4.4 The 3D EEG Data Tensor and Machine Learning Slicing Strategies

Once continuous multi-channel EEG is segmented into discrete trials (epochs), how is it stored in memory (e.g., in MATLAB or Python/NumPy)?

  

```
                     /|~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~/|
                    / |                        / |
     Trials (M)    /  |                       /  |
   (e.g., 100)    /   |                      /   |
                 /    |                     /    |
                +~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~+     |
                |     |                    |     |
                |     |                    |     |
   Channels (C) |     +--------------------|-----+
   (e.g., 32)   |    /                     |    /
                |   /                      |   /
                |  /                       |  /
                | /                        | /
                +~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~+/
                     Time / Samples (T)
                   (e.g., 400 samples)
```

- **The 3D EEG Data Cube (Tensor):** Segmented EEG data is universally formatted as a three-dimensional array (order-3 tensor) $\mathcal{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times T \times M}$ (or permuted as $M \times C \times T$ depending on the toolbox):
    
      
    1. **Dimension 1 — $Channels$ ($C$):** Spatial sensors ($c \in \{1, \dots, C\}$, e.g., $Cz, C_3, C_4$).
        
          
        
    2. **Dimension 2 — $Time$ / $Samples$ ($T$):** Discrete temporal sample points within a single trial epoch ($t \in \{1, \dots, T\}$).
        
          
        
    3. **Dimension 3 — $Trials$ ($M$):** Repetitions of the experimental task across the session ($m \in \{1, \dots, M\}$, where each trial carries a class label $y_m \in \{\text{Negative}, \text{Positive}\}$ or $\{\text{Left}, \text{Right}\}$).
        
          
        

Slides 10 through 13 of the lecture illustrate a brilliant analytical concept: **how we slice, flatten, or aggregate across the $3\text{D}$ tensor ($\text{Channel} \times \text{Time} \times \text{Trial}$) to perform classification and compute the Area Under the ROC Curve ($\text{AUC}$)** (e.g., distinguishing **Negative vs. Positive Emotion** with $\text{AUC}$ scores like $0.80, 0.81$):

  

#### Strategy A: Temporal Decoding (Time-Point-by-Time-Point Spatial Classification — Slide 10)

- **How it slices the Cube:** Fix a single time sample $t_k$ (or a tiny sliding window) and extract a $2\text{D}$ slice across all **Channels $\times$ Trials** ($\mathbf{X}_{:, t_k, :} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times M}$).
<br>
- **Classification Workflow:**
    
      
    - For that exact millisecond $t_k$, each trial $m$ is represented by a feature vector of length $C$ (the spatial voltage pattern across all $C$ channels at time $t_k$).
    <br>
    - Train a binary classifier (e.g., Linear Discriminant Analysis or SVM) across the $M$ trials to separate Class 1 (**Negative Emotion**) from Class 2 (**Positive Emotion**), and evaluate performance using the **Area Under the ROC Curve ($\text{AUC}$)**.
    <br>
    - Repeat this process independently for every time point $t_1, t_2, \dots, t_T$, yielding an **$\text{AUC}(t)$ time-course curve** (e.g., $\text{AUC} = 0.50$ at baseline, rising to $0.80$ at $t_1$ and $0.81$ at $t_2$ when emotional processing peaks in the cortex)!
<br>
- **Goal:** Answers the question _"At what exact millisecond after stimulus onset does the spatial pattern across the brain differentiate between conditions?"_
    
      
    

#### Strategy B: Spatial Decoding (Channel-by-Channel Temporal Classification — Slide 11)

- **How it slices the Cube:** Fix a single electrode channel $c_k$ and extract a $2\text{D}$ slice across **Time $\times$ Trials** ($\mathbf{X}_{c_k, :, :} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times M}$).
    
      
    
- **Classification Workflow:**
    
      
    - For that specific sensor $c_k$, each trial $m$ is represented by its temporal waveform vector of length $T$ (all time samples within the window).
        
          
        
    - Train a classifier across the $M$ trials using only channel $c_k$'s time series, computing a single $\text{AUC}_{c_k}$ score for that electrode.
        
          
        
    - Repeat for all $C$ channels and project the resulting $\text{AUC}$ scores onto a $2\text{D}$ **scalp topography map**.
        
          
        
- **Goal:** Answers the question _"Which specific anatomical brain regions (electrodes) carry the most discriminative temporal information for this task?"_
    
      
    

#### Strategy C: Spatio-Temporal Searchlight / Feature Matrix (Channel $\times$ Time Matrix — Slide 12 & 13)

- **How it slices the Cube:**
    
      
    - **Element-wise / Local Window Classification:** Evaluate classification performance ($\text{AUC}$) at every individual $(c, t)$ coordinate pair across the $M$ trials, producing a $2\text{D}$ **Channel $\times$ Time decoding heatmap** showing both _where_ and _when_ information emerges.
        
          
        
    - **Dimension Reduction / Summation Aggregation:** Alternatively, sum or average voltage/power across specific time windows ($\sum_t$) or channel clusters ($\sum_c$), or flatten the $C \times T$ matrix into a single long feature vector $\mathbf{x}_m \in \mathbb{R}^{(C \cdot T) \times 1}$ per trial for global spatio-temporal decoding.
        
          
        
- **Example:** In an emotion recognition study comparing **Negative vs. Positive** stimuli across $32\text{ channels}$ and $400\text{ time samples}$ ($4\text{ seconds}$ at $100\text{ Hz}$), running Strategy A reveals that $\text{AUC}$ jumps above $0.80$ specifically between $300\text{ ms}$ and $600\text{ ms}$ post-stimulus, while Strategy B reveals that frontal-asymmetry channels ($F_3, F_4$) drive this classification performance.
    
      
    

## 5. Mathematical Foundations of Spectral Analysis: Sine, Cosine, and Phase Geometry

Why does a BCI course spend the final section of a lecture dissecting right-angled triangles, unit circles, **sine** waves, and **cosine** waves? Because by **Joseph Fourier's Theorem**, _any_ arbitrary time-series signal—no matter how jagged or complex an EEG trace looks—can be represented as a weighted sum of pure **sine** and **cosine** waves of varying frequencies and amplitudes!

  

### 5.1 Trigonometric Foundations and Unit Circle Projection

Let us reconstruct the exact geometric derivation drawn on Slide 22:

  

```
                        Imaginary / Sine Axis (Opposite)
                                      ^
                                      |        * P (Rotating Vector Tip)
                                      |       /|
                                      |      / |
                                      | hyp /  | opp = hyp * sin(theta)
                                      | (A)/   |
                                      |   /    |
                                      |  /theta|
       -------------------------------+--------+-------------> Real / Cosine Axis
                                      |   adj = hyp * cos(theta)   (Adjacent)
                                      |
                                      |
```

- **Right-Triangle Trigonometry (SOH-CAH-TOA):**
    
      
    - Consider a vector of length **Hypotenuse ($\text{hyp}$)**—which in signal processing represents the **Magnitude** or peak **Amplitude ($A$)** of an oscillation—rotated at an angle $\theta$ (the **Phase** angle) relative to the horizontal reference axis:
        
          
        
        $$\cos(\theta) = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}} = \frac{\text{adj}}{\text{hyp}} \implies \text{adj} = \text{hyp} \cdot \cos(\theta)$$
        
        $$\sin(\theta) = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}} = \frac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}} \implies \text{opp} = \text{hyp} \cdot \sin(\theta)$$
        
    - When the rotating vector is a **Unit Vector** ($\text{hyp} = 1$), the horizontal projection is simply $\cos(\theta)$ and the vertical projection is simply $\sin(\theta)$.
        
          
        
- **The CAST Rule (Quadrant Sign Convention):**
    
      
    - As noted in the lower-left diagram of Slide 22 ("Cast"), as the angle $\theta$ rotates counter-clockwise through the four quadrants of the Cartesian plane ($0 \to 2\pi$ radians, or $0^\circ \to 360^\circ$):
        
          
        - **Quadrant I ($0 \text{ to } \frac{\pi}{2}$ — All / A):** Both $\sin(\theta) > 0$ and $\cos(\theta) > 0$.
            
              
            
        - **Quadrant II ($\frac{\pi}{2} \text{ to } \pi$ — Sine / S):** $\sin(\theta) > 0$, while $\cos(\theta) < 0$.
            
              
            
        - **Quadrant III ($\pi \text{ to } \frac{3\pi}{2}$ — Tangent / T):** Both $\sin(\theta) < 0$ and $\cos(\theta) < 0$ ($\tan(\theta) > 0$).
            
              
            
        - **Quadrant IV ($\frac{3\pi}{2} \text{ to } 2\pi$ — Cosine / C):** $\cos(\theta) > 0$, while $\sin(\theta) < 0$.
            
              
            
- **Generating Time-Domain Waves from Circular Rotation:**
    
      
    - Imagine a vector of length $A$ spinning counter-clockwise around a circle at a constant frequency of $f$ full revolutions (**cycles**) per second.
        
          
        
    - Since one complete cycle around a circle equals $2\pi\text{ radians}$ ($360^\circ$), the instantaneous angle $\theta(t)$ at time $t$ (with starting phase offset $\phi_0$) is:
        
          
        
        $$\theta(t) = 2\pi f t + \phi_0$$
        
    - Plotting the vertical shadow ($\text{opp}$) of that spinning vector over time $t$ traces a **Sine Wave**, and plotting its horizontal shadow ($\text{adj}$) traces a **Cosine Wave**:
        
          
        
        $$x_{\sin}(t) = A \sin(2\pi f t + \phi_0)$$
        
        $$x_{\cos}(t) = A \cos(2\pi f t + \phi_0)$$
        

### 5.2 Anatomy of a Sinusoidal Oscillation: Amplitude, Frequency, and Phase

Every neural oscillation is uniquely parameterized by three physical properties:

  

1. **Amplitude ($A$ / Magnitude):** The peak vertical excursion of the wave from its zero baseline (measured in microvolts, $\mu\text{V}$, for EEG), representing the degree of synchronous neural firing within that frequency band. (**Power** is proportional to amplitude squared: $P \propto A^2$).
    
      
    
2. **Frequency ($f$):** The number of complete oscillatory cycles ($2\pi\text{ radians}$) completed in $1\text{ second}$, measured in $\text{Hz}$ ($f = \frac{1}{T_{\text{period}}}$).
    
      
    
3. **Phase ($\phi$):** The fractional position of the wave within its $2\pi$ cycle at a specific reference time $t$.
    
      
    - **Why do we need _both_ Sine and Cosine in EEG analysis?**
        
          
        
    - Look at their starting values at $t = 0$ (assuming $\phi_0 = 0$):
        
          
        
        $$\cos(0) = 1 \quad (\text{starts at maximum peak})$$
        
        $$\sin(0) = 0 \quad (\text{starts at zero-crossing})$$
        
    - A **Cosine wave** is simply a **Sine wave shifted in phase by $+\frac{\pi}{2}\text{ radians}$ ($90^\circ$)**:
        
          
        
        $$\cos(\theta) = \sin\left(\theta + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$$
        
    - **Fundamental Law of Quadrature Phase Detection:** Because $\sin(\theta)$ and $\cos(\theta)$ are $90^\circ$ ($\frac{\pi}{2}\text{ radians}$) apart, they are **mutually orthogonal** (their inner product over a full period is zero: $\int_0^{2\pi} \sin(\theta)\cos(\theta)\,d\theta = 0$).
    <br>
    - **Example:** Suppose an EEG channel contains a pure $10\text{ Hz}$ Alpha wave that happens to behave like $\cos(2\pi \cdot 10 t)$. If you try to detect this $10\text{ Hz}$ wave by correlating (dot-producting) your EEG signal _only_ against a reference **Sine wave** $\sin(2\pi \cdot 10 t)$, the dot product will equal **zero** because the two waves are $90^\circ$ out of phase! You would falsely conclude there is zero Alpha activity in the brain!
   <br>
    - By projecting the EEG signal onto **both Cosine (Real axis) and Sine (Imaginary axis)** simultaneously via **Euler’s Formula** ($e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$), we capture the true **Magnitude ($A$)** and **Phase ($\theta$)** regardless of how the brain wave is shifted in time:
        
          
        
        $$c_{\cos} = \langle \text{EEG}(t), \cos(2\pi f t) \rangle, \quad c_{\sin} = \langle \text{EEG}(t), \sin(2\pi f t) \rangle$$
        
        $$\text{Amplitude } A(f) = \sqrt{c_{\cos}^2 + c_{\sin}^2}, \quad \text{Phase } \theta(f) = \arctan2(c_{\sin}, c_{\cos})$$
        
    - Look back at Slide 22—that right triangle with $\text{adj} = c_{\cos}$, $\text{opp} = c_{\sin}$, and $\text{hyp} = A$ is the exact geometric heart of the **Fourier Transform** and **Wavelet Convolution**!
        
          
        

### 5.3 The Gabor-Heisenberg Time-Frequency Uncertainty Principle

Slide 17 highlights a critical theoretical boundary in signal processing: **Time-Frequency Uncertainty**.

  

- **The Time-Frequency Uncertainty Principle (Gabor Limit)** states that a signal cannot be simultaneously localized with arbitrary precision in both the **time domain ($\Delta t$)** and the **frequency domain ($\Delta f$)**:
    
      
    
    $$\Delta t \cdot \Delta f \ge \frac{1}{4\pi}$$
    
- **Physical Intuition:**
    
      
    - To know the **exact time** an event occurs ($\Delta t \to 0$), you must use a very short time window. However, a very short time window cannot even contain one full cycle of a slow wave (e.g., a $2\text{ Hz}$ Delta wave takes $500\text{ ms}$ just to complete one cycle!), making your frequency resolution terrible ($\Delta f$ is huge).
        
          
        
    - Conversely, to distinguish between $10.0\text{ Hz}$ and $10.2\text{ Hz}$ with **high frequency precision** ($\Delta f \to 0$), you must observe the signal over a long time window (several seconds), which averages out and blurs the exact millisecond the oscillation started or stopped ($\Delta t$ is huge).
        
          
        
- **Wavelet Transform Solution (Morlet Wavelets):**
    
      
    - Rather than using a fixed window length like the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT), **Wavelet Analysis** (implemented via the Wavelet Toolbox noted on Slide 18) tapers a complex sinusoid ($e^{i 2\pi f t} = \cos(2\pi f t) + i\sin(2\pi f t)$) with a Gaussian envelope, scaling the window duration dynamically with frequency: using **wide time windows for low frequencies** (high $\Delta f$ precision) and **narrow time windows for high frequencies** (high $\Delta t$ precision).
        
          
        
- **Analogy:** Think of musical notation. To identify a deep bass organ note ($20\text{ Hz}$), your ear must listen to the vibration for a noticeable fraction of a second, making it hard to pinpoint the exact millisecond it started. Conversely, a sharp snare drum click occurs at an exact millisecond in time, but spreads energy across all frequencies so it has no single discernible pitch.
    
      
    

### 5.4 Computational Implementation (`Lively_Vectors` & MATLAB/Python Ecosystem)

Slide 18 and 19 bridge these mathematical concepts to practical code execution, referencing essential toolboxes (Signal Processing, Wavelet, Statistics & Machine Learning, Deep Learning) and Dr. Mahmoud Abdellahi’s open-source **`Lively_Vectors` (LV)** toolbox (`cosine_sine_visualisation.m`).

  

To solidify how `cosine_sine_visualisation.m` works under the hood, here is the clean mathematical workflow (expressed in both MATLAB and Python/NumPy syntax so you can run or visualize it immediately):

  



```Matlab
% MATLAB Equivalent of cosine_sine_visualisation.m concepts
Fs = 100;                   % Sampling rate: 100 Hz (Slide 16)
dt = 1 / Fs;                % Sampling interval: 0.01 sec (10 ms)
t = 0 : dt : 2 - dt;        % 2-second time vector (200 samples)

f = 2;                      % Oscillation frequency: 2 Hz (2 cycles/sec)
A = 1.5;                    % Amplitude (hypotenuse / magnitude)
phi = pi / 4;               % Initial phase offset (45 degrees)

% Instantaneous phase angle theta(t)
theta = 2 * pi * f * t + phi;

% Project rotating vector onto Cosine (adjacent) and Sine (opposite) axes
x_cos = A * cos(theta);     % Real component
x_sin = A * sin(theta);     % Imaginary / Quadrature component

% Recover instantaneous Magnitude (hypotenuse) and Phase at any sample
recovered_amp   = sqrt(x_cos.^2 + x_sin.^2); % Always equals A (1.5)
recovered_phase = atan2(x_sin, x_cos);       % Instantaneous angle in [-pi, pi]
```

## Professor's Synthesis

If you take away one overarching principle from today's lecture, let it be this: **An EEG signal is never a direct, raw readout of a single brain region—it is a relativized, volume-conducted, spatio-temporal projection**.

  

Every analytical choice you make alters what you see:

  

1. **Your choice of Spatial Reference (Montage)** acts as a spatial lens: a **$Cz$ reference** blinds you to vertex activity; **CAR** strips away global common-mode noise across the head; and a **Laplacian montage** acts as a spatial microscope that sharpens superficial radial sources directly beneath an electrode while erasing deep, broad signals.
<br>
2. **Your choice of Tensor Slicing ($Channel \times Time \times Trial$)** determines your scientific hypothesis: slicing across channels at a fixed millisecond tests _when_ a mental state emerges, whereas slicing across time at a single electrode tests _where_ that state resides.
  <br>
3. **Your choice of Spectral Basis (Sine & Cosine Quadrature)** determines whether you can survive **trial jitter**: when cognitive responses aren't strictly locked to the exact millisecond of a stimulus, simple time-domain averaging fails, but projecting the signal onto orthogonal **$\cos(\theta)$** and **$\sin(\theta)$** axes allows you to extract phase-invariant oscillatory power ($A = \sqrt{\text{adj}^2 + \text{opp}^2}$) across Delta, Theta, Alpha, Beta, and Gamma bands.
    
      
    

Master the geometry of the unit circle and the linear algebra of the $3\text{D}$ data cube, and you have mastered the engine room of every modern Brain-Computer Interface.
