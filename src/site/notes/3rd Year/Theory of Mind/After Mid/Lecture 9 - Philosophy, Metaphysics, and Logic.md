### 1. Introduction to Philosophy and Epistemology

The lecture begins by outlining the four main branches of modern philosophy: **Epistemology, Metaphysics, Logic, and Ethics**.

- **Epistemology:** Derived from the Greek word _epistēmē_ (knowledge), this is the "Theory of Knowledge." It studies the nature, origin, and limits of human knowledge.
    
- Philosophers study epistemology using "Introspection" (examining one's own mental and emotional states) and "Experimental Philosophy" (learning from reproducible experiments).
    

### 2. Physics vs. Metaphysics (Aristotle's Perspective)

Aristotle, one of the founding fathers of Western Philosophy, is considered the founder of Metaphysics.

- **Physics:** To Aristotle, this was the philosophy of physical objects in nature that can be directly sensed and changed (e.g., the building blocks of physical objects and their movements).
    
- **Metaphysics:** Written after his books on nature, this deals with non-physical phenomena that cannot be directly sensed. It explores abstract ideas like the "Nature of Existence," how the universe was created, the concept of Free Will versus Fate, and the Mind-Body Problem.
    

### 3. The Mind-Body Problem

A central metaphysical question is whether the "mind" (cognition, affect, and conation) and the physical "body" (the brain) are separate entities. The lecture divides the theories into three main camps:

**A. Monism (One Entity):** The belief that there is only one substance in the world.

- **Physicalism:** The belief that only physical things exist, meaning the brain and nervous system perform all activities of the mind.
    <br>
    - **Mechanism:** A foundational Physicalist theory stating the material world is composed of small particles (atoms, ions, cells), and their arrangement explains everything.
        <br>
    - **Mind-Brain Identity Theory:** Proposes that every mental state is identical to a specific physical state (e.g., specific ion channel activity always equals a specific feeling of pressure or happiness). However, this theory is criticized because identical mental states (like happiness or pain) can be generated by vastly different physical states, conditions, or even different species.
        <br>
    - **Functionalism:** Compares the human body to computer hardware (neurons/synapses as circuits) and the mind to computer software. It uses an Input-Processing-Output (IPO) approach to reverse-engineer the mind. For example, an input (a burn) leads to processing (fear/pain), which leads to an output (pulling your hand away).

- **Idealism**: only “Minds” exist, containing ideas (this is an extreme view that is heavily criticized and only few people agree with) 

**B. Dualism (Two Entities):** The belief that the physical body and the non-physical mind/soul are completely separate. René Descartes argued for this, noting that thoughts cannot be physically touched or located in a specific body part.

- **Interactionism:** The mind and body are distinct but interact directly (e.g., the mind wants a mango, so it causes the physical arm to move).
    <br>
- **Occasionalism:** The mind and body do not interact directly; instead, actions are an occasion for God to make the physical body move.
    

**C. Behaviorism:**

The study of the mind strictly through observable actions. Some behaviorists believe the mind doesn't exist at all, while others believe it exists but is entirely unobservable, making behavior the only valid metric of study.

### 4. The "Hard Problem" of Consciousness

Coined by philosopher David Chalmers in 1995, this highlights a major limitation in understanding the mind.

- **The "Easy" Problem:** Explaining the physical structures (neurons, ion gates) and functional IPO mechanisms of the brain.
    
- **The "Hard" Problem:** Explaining _why_ an organism is conscious in the first place, and the highly subjective experience of "what it is like to be" that specific organism.
    

### 5. Logic, Inconsistency, and Gödel's Theorems

The final section uses the branch of **Logic** to ask if cognitive architecture projects can perfectly model the human mind using mathematical axioms.

- **Axioms:** An axiom is a statement that everyone believes is true , such as: "the only constant is change.” Mathematicians use the word axiom to refer to an established proof. “Axioms” are the Building blocks of Math and Logic
<br>
- **Inconsistent Problems:** Logic frequently runs into infinite loops or "decidability problems," such as the Liar Paradox ("This statement is false"), Epimenides the Cretan claiming "All Cretans are liars," or a contradictory loop about who is a friend versus an enemy.
    <br>
- **Gödel's Incompleteness Theorems:** Kurt Gödel proved that any complex system of logical axioms will inevitably contain statements that can neither be proved nor disproved by the system itself, meaning the system will always be incomplete.
    <br>
- **John Lucas vs. Mechanism:** In 1959, philosopher John Lucas used Gödel's theorem to argue against the theory of Mechanism. He stated that if scientists built a perfect algorithmic computer simulation of a mathematician's brain based on axioms, that computer would be unable to prove certain formulas due to Gödel's theorem. However, the _human_ mathematician could step outside the system and see that the formula is true. Therefore, Lucas concluded that a machine can never fully and exactly represent the human mind.
