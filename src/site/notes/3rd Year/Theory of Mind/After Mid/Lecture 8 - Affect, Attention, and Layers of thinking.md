The lecture "Affect, Attention & Layers of Thinking" explores how the human mind processes emotions, manages focus, and delegates tasks across different levels of consciousness. It is divided into three primary themes.

### 1. Affect and Emotions

The first section breaks down the internal feeling states known as "affect," which can be categorized as either positive or negative.

- **Positive Affect:** This feeling state occurs when a goal is attained, a source of threat is avoided, or an individual is satisfied with their present state of affairs.
    
- **Negative Affect:** Conversely, this occurs when an individual fails to attain a goal, fails to avoid a threat, or is not satisfied with their present state.
    

Emotions can be mapped along two main dimensions:

- **Valence:** Whether the emotion brings pleasure (Positive Valence) or displeasure (Negative Valence).
    
- **Arousal (Intensity):** The level of activation or deactivation, which sometimes correlates with adrenaline levels in the body. For example, being "Excited" combines high pleasure with high arousal, while being "Gloomy" combines high displeasure with low arousal. Furthermore, each emotion has a spectrum of intensity; for instance, happiness ranges from mild sensory pleasure to ecstasy, and anger ranges from annoyance to extreme fury.
    

The lecture highlights the work of Paul Ekman, who identified 7 Basic Emotions: Happiness, Sadness, Surprise, Fear, Disgust, Contempt, and Anger. These emotions are linked to specific facial muscle movements, known as micro expressions, which occur subconsciously and can reveal hidden feelings. An AI analysis of 6 million videos across 144 countries demonstrated that these exact facial expressions occur in similar contexts worldwide, proving they are a cross-cultural biological phenomenon.

### 2. Attention and Cognitive Stress

The second section demonstrates how attention works by showing the difference between processing normal inputs versus conflicting inputs. When reading words where the text color conflicts with the word's meaning (e.g., the word "BLUE" printed in red ink), it takes longer to process because the conflicting data forces the conscious mind to intervene and work harder to resolve the mental conflict.

Deep thinking and high concentration place measurable physical stress on the body. When the conscious mind is operating near its maximum capacity—such as when holding the maximum 5 to 7 items in short-term memory—the body secretes stress hormones like adrenaline. A visible physical indicator of this cognitive overload is the dilation (widening) of the pupils during intense mental tasks.

### 3. Layers of Mental Activities

The final section explains how the brain manages complex tasks over time by dividing activities into different layers of control. When learning a difficult new skill, such as driving, speaking a new language, or performing surgery, a person must strictly use their conscious mind. This requires intense concentration, leaving no cognitive space for distractions like talking.

However, with enough training, the brain undergoes a process of delegation. It "pushes" the processing of these familiar tasks down into the subconscious mind. This relieves stress on the conscious brain and frees it up to handle other activities simultaneously.

The brain categorizes mental control into three distinct levels:

- **Conscious Control:** Requires significant attention, effort, and concentration, such as doing math or learning a brand-new skill.
    
- **Subconscious Control:** Tasks that are initially difficult but become automatic after sufficient training and repetition, like driving a familiar route or speaking a practiced language.
    
- **Unconscious Control:** Hard-wired, automatic processes that humans are born with, such as heart rate, respiratory rate, and the physical reflex of withdrawing a hand from a painful stimulus.
